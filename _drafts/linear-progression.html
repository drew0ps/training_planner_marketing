Training methods

Linear progression: add weight every time you get all your reps

Get every rep, and next time the bar is a step heavier. Miss, and it stays where it was until you get them all. It's the simplest rule in strength training, and in the first year it's hard to beat.

Not published yet · 4 min read

The rule

Each exercise has a weight, a number of sets and a number of reps: three sets of five at 60 kg, say. Get all fifteen reps and the next time you do that exercise it's 62.5 kg. Get fourteen and it's 60 kg again, until you get all fifteen. That's all of it.

It works because a beginner recovers from a workout and comes back stronger within a few days. Adding a little every time keeps the work just ahead of what you can already do, which is progressive overload at its most direct.

How big the steps are

Small enough that you'll get the reps most of the time. The usual steps:

  • 2.5 kg (5 lb) for the squat, the bench press and the row.
  • 5 kg (10 lb) for the deadlift, which is the strongest lift and moves fastest.
  • 1.25 kg (2.5 lb) for the overhead press, which stalls first: the shoulders are small muscles and the press is a slow lift to grow.

Smaller steps go further. A lift that gains 1.25 kg every time for twenty workouts gains 25 kg; one that tries for 5 kg and misses every other time gains less, and it feels worse. If your gym has no 0.5 kg or 1.25 kg plates, tell the app which plates it has and the steps will be the ones you can load.

When it stops working

Eventually you miss, and miss again at the same weight. One miss means little: a short night, a bad day. Three in a row at the same weight means the lift has caught up with the plan. Then you have three choices, in this order:

  1. Rest. A deload lets the fatigue of weeks of heavy work come down, and the weight often moves again after it.
  2. Back off and climb again. Take about 10 % off that lift and add the steps again from there. The second climb usually goes past the old sticking point.
  3. Change how you progress. When even that stops working, you're no longer a beginner, and adding weight every workout has done its job. Progress comes over weeks now: double progression, effort-based progression, or a program that moves in waves like Strength Wave.

Set it up in TrainingPlanner

Progression is set per exercise, so a program can move its main lifts linearly and its accessories by double progression. A program's Progression page lists every exercise with its model; set one for all of them at once, or open an exercise to give it its own.

  1. Open the program in Programs and tap Progression.
  2. Tap the model at the top to set it for every exercise: pick Linear. Undo is right there if you change your mind.
  3. Tap an exercise to give it its own: the model, whether it adds weight or reps, and the Step.
  4. New exercises take the program's default progression; you set that when you create a program.

In the workout you don't count anything. The first set starts at the weight the rule gives, with the reason beside it: "All reps completed last time: +2.5 kg", or "Missed the target last time: repeat 60 kg × 5". A set logged at a lighter weight than planned carries the lighter weight on to the next sets, since it was what the day allowed.

Free or Pro. In the free app every set starts from what you lifted last time and you add the step yourself. The progression models, the steps added for you and the next cycle written from your log are part of TrainingPlanner Pro.

Programs that use it

  • 5×5 Strength: five sets of five, three workouts a week.
  • Full Body A/B: three sets of five, every other day.
  • Tiered Full Body: a heavy lift, a volume lift and an accessory in each workout.
  • 10×10 Volume: the weight goes up once all ten sets reach ten reps.
  • Pyramid Upper · Lower: each step of the pyramid moves up once you get all its reps.
