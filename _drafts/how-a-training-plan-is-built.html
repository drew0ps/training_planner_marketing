Training methods

Microcycles, mesocycles and macrocycles: how a training plan is built

Coaches plan in nested loops: days make a cycle, cycles make a phase, phases make a program. Each level has a job. Once you see them, every program reads the same way, and building your own gets a lot easier.

Not published yet · 4 min read

The three levels

A cycle (coaches say microcycle) is the smallest unit that repeats: the sessions on their days, and the rest days between them. Most people think in weeks, and a week is a fine cycle, but it doesn't have to be one. Push, legs, pull and a rest day is a four-day cycle; two workouts that take turns three times a week repeat every fourteen days.

A phase (a mesocycle, or block) is a run of cycles with one focus: more volume, heavier loads, or a peak. Inside a phase the cycles are alike but not the same; the targets climb from one to the next, and the last is often a deload. Four to eight weeks is typical.

A program (the macrocycle) is the phases in order, toward a goal: a strength test, a summer, a season. A few months, usually, after which you look back at what moved and choose what comes next.

Why cycles don't have to be weeks

A rotation of sessions has its own rhythm. Push, legs, pull and a day off gives each session every fourth day, which is more often than a week allows with three sessions and less often than six days in a row. Tie it to the week and you either train on the same weekdays and wait longer, or squeeze the rotation into seven days.

TrainingPlanner lets a cycle be anything from 2 to 14 days, and counts it from the day you start, not from Monday. The calendar then shows each session on its real date. Programs built around the week stay at seven days; the ones built on a rotation keep their own rhythm.

Two kinds of plan

Not every plan needs phases. TrainingPlanner has two kinds:

  • A repeating plan is one cycle that repeats until you change it. The weights and reps still progress from workout to workout; the plan itself doesn't change. It's how most people start, and for a beginner it's often all a plan needs to be.
  • A structured plan is phases of cycles, each phase with its focus and its targets climbing from cycle to cycle, deloads where they belong, and a periodization model if you want one to write the targets. It's what an intermediate or advanced lifter needs, when adding a little every workout has stopped working.

A repeating plan can grow into a structured one at any time: open it and tap Turn into a structured plan. The cycles so far become its first phase.

Build a repeating plan

  1. In Programs, tap + › Build My Own › Repeating Plan.
  2. Name it, then start from scratch (pick the training days) or from a cycle: one of yours or a template's.
  3. Plan the sessions on the board: drag them to other days, add exercises with +, and tap a card to set its sets, reps, rest and progression.
  4. Start it from Today.

The quick start builds one this way, step by step: a simple Push · Legs · Pull, three days a week.

Build a structured plan

A structured plan is set up in five steps on one sheet, under a picture of the plan that changes as you go: the cycle, the periodization, the phases, the default progression, and a name.

  1. In Programs, tap + › Build My Own › Structured Plan.
  2. Cycle: plan a new one on the board after this sheet, or take one of yours or a template's. Its length and days come with it.
  3. Periodization: pick a model, or turn on Automatic and say your goal, experience and how many cycles you want.
  4. Phases: how many cycles in each, and whether the last cycle of each is a deload.
  5. Default progression for new exercises, and a name. Then Create.

The phases share one cycle by default, so you plan it once on the board and every phase follows. When a phase needs its own, open it and turn off Same cycle in every phase. To change one cycle only, for a heavier week or an easier one, pick it in the chips above the board's columns and change its targets there.
