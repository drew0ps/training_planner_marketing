Training methods

Periodization explained: planning how training changes over time

Doing the same workout forever stops working, and changing it at random doesn't work either. Periodization is the middle way: a plan for how the training changes, phase by phase, so each one builds on the last.

Not published yet · 4 min read

Why plan the changes

A beginner improves on almost anything, as long as it's a little harder each time. After a year or two that stops being true. Heavy work builds strength, but heavy work every week wears you down; lots of volume builds muscle, but you can't keep adding sets forever. Different qualities need different training, and the body adapts to whatever it's given until it has nothing left to adapt to.

Periodization splits the program into phases, each with a focus, and plans the move from one to the next: a phase of volume to build muscle and work capacity, then heavier phases that turn it into strength, with easier cycles in between to let the fatigue come down. Coaches have argued for decades about which way of doing it is best. The honest answer is that the plan matters more than the model, and any of them beats no plan once you're past the beginner stage.

The six models

TrainingPlanner has six models, each described in plain words when you pick one, with a chart of its loads. They differ in how they move between the qualities they train:

What a model writes

Each training quality starts from a prescription, and the model decides which one every cycle (or, for daily undulating, every session) gets:

FocusSets × repsLoadEffortRest
Hypertrophy4 × 1070 % of 1RMRPE 890 s
Strength5 × 580 %RPE 83 min
Power6 × 275 %RPE 62 min
Peaking4 × 288 %RPE 94 min
Endurance3 × 1560 %RPE 71 min

Inside a phase the load climbs 2.5 % of your max per cycle (never past 95 %), and from the third cycle the sets aim a point harder. With deloads on, the last cycle of every phase of three or more cycles is a deload: half the work sets, at least two, ten points lighter (70 % of your max becomes 60 %), at RPE 6.

Two things keep a model from flattening your program:

  • Exercises that progress by themselves (linear, double progression, RPE-based) start each phase at that phase's first load and move up from what you log, rather than following the model's climb. Only exercises set to As planned follow the model's loads to the letter. Exercises on double progression get a rep range around the prescribed reps: 8–12 for the hypertrophy tens.
  • Accessories keep their own sets. Mark an exercise as an accessory and the model leaves it alone: the curls stay at 3 × 12 while the squats wave.

Loads come from each exercise's one-rep max, or an estimate from its planned sets until you enter one.

Apply a model in TrainingPlanner

  1. Open a structured plan in Programs and scroll to Periodization.
  2. Tap Model and pick one. Each shows its chart: a bar per session, its height the planned load, its colour what it trains.
  3. Back on the program, the chart and an outline say what each phase gets. Turn the deloads on or off.
  4. Tap Apply to all cycles and confirm. Every cycle's sets, reps and loads are written from the model; the phases are renamed. Undo is right below if you change your mind.

Picking a model writes nothing by itself; the program says whether its cycles follow the model, and Apply to all cycles makes them. A phase can also be grown from its first cycle, with Progress from cycle 1 in the phase's page, for a plan you write by hand.

Let the app choose

When you build a structured plan, turn on Automatic under Periodization and say your goal, your experience and how many cycles you want, from 4 to 24. TrainingPlanner picks the model, the length of each phase, the deloads and the default progression, and says why: a beginner gets a straight line, an intermediate after strength gets blocks, an advanced one the pendulum; size goes to undulation.
