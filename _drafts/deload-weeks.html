Deloads: why the plan has easier weeks in it

Hard training builds fitness and fatigue at the same time, and the fatigue hides the fitness. A deload is a planned easier week or two that lets the fatigue go and the fitness show. It isn't a week off; it's part of the program.

Not published yet · 4 min read

Fitness and fatigue

After a hard week you're fitter than before, and more tired. For a while the gains outrun the fatigue and the weights keep moving. But fatigue builds up over weeks in ways a night's sleep doesn't clear: joints ache, the bar feels slow, the same weights take more effort. Keep pushing and the lifts fall back, not because you got weaker, but because you're too tired to show it.

A deload cuts the work for a cycle. The fatigue drops faster than the fitness does, and when the hard training starts again, the fitness is there to build on. Lifters often hit their best numbers in the cycle after a deload.

What a deload looks like

Lighter, shorter, and nowhere near failure. TrainingPlanner deloads a cycle like this:

  • Half the work sets of each exercise, at least one.
  • About 10 % less weight, rounded to plates you can load; sets in percentages of your max come down by a tenth too.
  • No drop sets, rest-pause or myo-reps, and no sets to failure.
  • A pyramid planned set by set keeps every other set, lighter: its shape at half the volume.

Where a periodization model writes the cycles, its deload is half the work sets (at least two), ten points lighter in percent of your max, at an easy RPE 6.

Planned deloads

In a structured plan, the last cycle of each phase can be a deload. It's on by default for every phase of three cycles or more, which is most of them: a phase of four weeks ends in a deload week, every fourth or fifth week of the program is easy. The built-in programs come with their deloads planned.

  1. Open a structured plan in Programs. Under Phases, open a phase to see its cycles; the deload is marked.
  2. To turn deloads on or off for every phase, use Deload in each phase's last cycle, when you create the program or under its periodization.
  3. On the board, pick the deload in the chips above the columns to see its lighter sets, or to change them.
  4. To make any other cycle a deload, open it from its phase and turn on Deload cycle.

Deloads you didn't plan

Sometimes the fatigue arrives early: a bad month at work, a new baby, a cold. If you log how hard your sets were, TrainingPlanner watches the last three cycles for the pattern that means you need rest rather than more effort: the average effort of your work sets climbing cycle after cycle, by at least a third of a point, while in the latest cycle at least two lifts fell back (their best estimated max down by more than 2 %) and more fell than went up.

When it sees that, Today says so, with the numbers: "Effort climbed from RPE 7.6 to 8.4 over cycles 3 to 5, and 4 lifts fell back while 1 went up." One tap makes the next cycle a deload; its targets are lightened like any other deload's. Or dismiss it and carry on, if you know why the week was hard.

Readiness can point the same way. When your training load runs well over your usual week, the readiness card says a lighter week or a deload soon would let it come down, and on a low day the session itself is eased. More on that in readiness and today's adjustment.

Free or Pro. Structured plans, with their planned deloads, are part of TrainingPlanner Pro. The suggestion from your log comes up in any program, the free app's repeating plan too; a repeating plan has no next cycle of its own to lighten, so take the easier week yourself.

How often

Every four to eight weeks of hard training is the usual range: more often for heavy strength work and older lifters, less often for lighter training and beginners, who may not need planned deloads at all. The best sign is the one the app looks for: effort going up while the weights don't.
