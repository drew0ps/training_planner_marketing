Training methods

Full body, upper/lower or push/pull/legs: choosing a training split

A split is how the training is divided between the days. The best one isn't the one the strongest person in your gym follows; it's the one that fits the days you can really train and gives each muscle enough work, often enough.

Not published yet · 4 min read

What matters in a split

Two numbers decide most of what a split does for you. The first is volume: how many hard sets each muscle gets in a week. Growth follows it, up to a point; ten to twenty hard sets a week is a good range for most muscles and most people. The second is frequency: how often a muscle is trained. Spreading the same sets over two sessions instead of one tends to work better, because the last sets of a long session for one muscle are tired sets.

Everything else is fit: how many days you have, how long a session can be, what equipment you have, and what you enjoy enough to keep doing. A split you follow for a year beats a better one you drop in a month.

The splits

Full body

Every session trains the whole body: a squat or a hinge, a press, a pull, and a little more. Two or three sessions a week train every muscle two or three times. It's the best start for a beginner, the best use of two or three days for anyone, and it forgives a missed day, because the next session covers the same ground.

Upper and lower

The upper body one day, the legs the next, twice a week: four sessions, every muscle twice, and each session shorter than a full-body one. Often one upper and one lower day is heavier and the other lighter. A good split for the second year and beyond.

Push, pull, legs

Pressing muscles (chest, shoulders, triceps) one day, pulling muscles (back, biceps, rear shoulders) the next, legs the third. Run through once a week it trains each muscle once; run through twice, in six days, it's one of the highest-volume splits there is. Run as push, legs, pull and a rest day, it trains each muscle every fourth day.

One muscle group a day

Chest one day, back the next, and so on through the week: lots of sets for one muscle in one session, each muscle once a week. It works when the volume is there, and it's how many people enjoy training, but it leaves a muscle untouched for six days, and a missed day costs a whole muscle's week.

Equipment narrows it further. With no equipment at all, Bodyweight at Home; with a pull-up bar and dip bars, Bodyweight Strength; with dumbbells and a bench, Dumbbell Full Body; with kettlebells, Kettlebell Full Body. The barbell programs need a rack, a bench and plates; the rest want a gym with cables and machines.

Let the app choose

If you'd rather not read the table, answer a few questions. TrainingPlanner leaves out what's above your level or needs equipment you don't have, ranks the rest by how well they match your level, your days and your goal, and says why each one fits.

  1. In Programs, tap + › Answer a Few Questions. (On a first launch, setup starts by itself.)
  2. About you: your experience and your goal.
  3. Your week: your equipment, how many days you can train and about how long a session can be.
  4. Your program: a repeating or a structured plan, then the recommendation, with other good fits below it.
  5. Check the cycle, name it and start today or later.

Simple rotations bend to your week: ask for four days and a three-session rotation is spread over days 1, 2, 4 and 5 of a seven-day cycle. Programs built around a week of their own keep it.
